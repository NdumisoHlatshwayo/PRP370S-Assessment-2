Module: PRP370S · Project: RideConnect · Role recorded in charter: Wireframes Designer

1. Agile Team Charter — Reflection

My role in the RideConnect project was recorded as Wireframes Designer. The charter helped define responsibilities and gave our team a basis for coordinating different parts of the project. In the group chat, I shared Figma wireframes on 23 May 2026 and asked for feedback. This illustrates how my design work depended on communication with other team members rather than working in isolation. I learned that agreeing on roles is only the starting point: we also need to keep each other informed, review work together, and clarify how design decisions affect the rest of the application.

2. User Stories and Tasks — Reflection

Wireframing helped me think about the RideConnect application from the perspective of its users. The project includes driver and rider interactions, such as registration, ride offers, requests and booking-related screens. By creating and sharing wireframes, I contributed to discussing how users might move through the application before all features were implemented. The group discussion about driver and passenger views shows why it was important to distinguish the needs of these two user types. I learned that a screen should be designed around a clear user goal, not simply around its appearance. The exact user stories I personally wrote or edited still need to be checked against Trello.

3. Product Backlog — Reflection

The team used Trello to organize the RideConnect backlog, and the group circulated an updated board for review on 30 July 2026. From a design perspective, I could use the proposed features to understand which screens and interactions needed attention. Sharing wireframes made it easier for the team to discuss how planned features might work in practice. I learned that backlog items and interface designs should be aligned: if a feature is unclear, the design can expose questions that the team needs to resolve. I still need to verify whether I personally changed any backlog priorities before making that claim.

4. Sprint Backlog — Reflection

On 23 July 2026, I volunteered for Sprint 3, which the group identified as UX Design. The team also discussed other sprint areas, including planning, user journeys, backend, frontend and testing. This showed me how UX design fits into a larger sequence of collaborative work. I learned that sprint planning should turn broad goals into specific tasks that teammates can understand and review. My documented responsibility was UX Design, but the exact tasks completed and sprint dates must be confirmed from the team's Trello records.

5. Agile Logbook — Reflection

The group chat provides a timeline of collaboration, including the sharing of Figma wireframes in May, sprint planning in July, backlog review in July and Expo build updates in September. Looking back at these updates helps me see how the project moved from discussing ideas and user experiences toward an application prototype. My own documented involvement includes sharing wireframes for feedback and volunteering for the UX Design sprint. I learned the value of keeping dated records of decisions and progress because they make it easier to explain what happened during a project. For a stronger personal logbook, I should add the specific feedback I received, changes I made and tasks I completed.

Individual reflection / WhatsApp group evidence

What working with the RideConnect team taught me

Working on RideConnect helped me understand that Agile teamwork depends on clear communication, shared responsibility and regular feedback, not simply dividing tasks between group members. The WhatsApp messages show that the team used Microsoft Teams links and meeting reminders to coordinate work in April, discussed how to organise meetings, shared design resources, and later circulated the Trello backlog. This gave us ways to stay connected, but the conversation also showed how easily plans and updates can become scattered across different channels.

From my own messages, I can point to specific moments where I tried to participate constructively. On 20 April 2026, I asked what I should prepare, showing that I wanted to understand expectations before contributing. On 23 May 2026, I shared Figma wireframes and requested feedback, which helped me appreciate that a design improves when other people can question and review it. On 23 July 2026, I volunteered for the UX Design work area when the team discussed sprint responsibilities. Those are documented contributions rather than claims that I personally completed every group task.

One difficulty was keeping track of decisions and progress. Some messages asked whether meetings were taking place, while another update indicated that a teammate would brief me after a meeting. This reminded me that not everyone always has the same information at the same time. I learned that asking questions early, documenting decisions, and following up on feedback are important for avoiding misunderstandings and duplicated effort.

Overall, I value the experience because it strengthened my confidence in sharing design work, communicating with teammates and taking responsibility for a defined contribution. If I worked on a similar project again, I would suggest a short shared meeting summary after each discussion, clearer task owners and acceptance criteria on Trello, and a simple record of what feedback was received and what changed as a result. That would make collaboration more transparent and help us evaluate our progress more fairly.
